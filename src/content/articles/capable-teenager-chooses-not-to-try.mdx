I have spent nearly twenty years with young people in exactly these years, in schools and in family homes, and I want to tell you what I see instead of the word most parents arrive with: lazy.

A capable teenager who stops trying on purpose is almost never lazy. They are protecting themselves. If they revise properly and the grade still comes back low, that grade means something about them. If they do not revise at all, it means nothing. It was never a fair test of what they could do. The shrug is not indifference. It is armour, and it is remarkably effective armour, because it is very hard to argue with a teenager who has genuinely stopped caring whether the result was good.

## Why this looks different from younger avoidance

Younger children who are struggling tend to show it: tears, tummy aches, visible distress. By Years 7 to 9, most young people have learned that visible distress gets noticed and managed by adults in ways they find uncomfortable or babyish. So the avoidance goes underground. It becomes quiet. Homework that was never mentioned rather than refused outright. A dropped subject at options rather than a tantrum about a difficult one. It is quiet enough that everyone around you, including teachers who see thirty other children a day, tells you it is just the age.

It is not just the age. It is a specific strategy, chosen, often not even consciously, because it works better than the alternative of trying and failing publicly.

## What doesn't help

Threats and consequences tend to address the behaviour (not trying) without touching the reason underneath it (fear of what trying and failing would mean). You can often force short-term compliance this way. You rarely restore the willingness to actually risk trying, because the underlying fear hasn't moved: you have simply made not trying more costly than trying, without making trying feel any safer.

## What actually helps

Start by naming the pattern gently and without accusation: "I wonder if part of you thinks that if you don't really try, it doesn't count if it goes wrong." Many teenagers, when this is said plainly and without judgement, visibly relax, because someone has finally described the actual thing, instead of describing the behaviour on the surface.

From there, the work is about lowering the stakes of trying, not raising the stakes of not trying. Small, private wins (a single piece of work attempted properly, with the result kept between the two of you rather than compared to a class average) start to prove that trying and getting it wrong is survivable. That evidence has to be built slowly and specifically. It cannot be argued into existence in one conversation.

## The same pattern, a different age

This is the same protective logic that, two years later, looks like a Year 11 who has not opened a revision guide since October. It has simply had longer to harden by then, and there is a countdown behind every conversation you try to have about it. Years 7 to 9 is the easier point to interrupt this pattern, not because the feeling is smaller, but because there is more time before it calcifies.

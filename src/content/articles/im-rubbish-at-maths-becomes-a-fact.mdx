For a while, it sounds like ordinary complaining. "Maths is boring." "I hate fractions." Every child says some version of this at some point, and mostly it passes. Then, somewhere around Year 5, the sentence quietly changes shape. It stops being about the subject and starts being about them: "I'm just bad at maths." Said flatly, almost bored, like a fact that was settled a long time ago and no longer needs discussing.

That shift is worth noticing, because a complaint and a belief behave very differently.

## Why this age is when it tends to happen

Year 5 and 6 is when comparison arrives properly. Children this age have usually spent a few years not paying close attention to how they measure up against classmates. Now they do. Tables get discussed. Marks get compared, even quietly, even without adults intending it. A child who has been finding a subject effortful for a while now has the social information to conclude that the effort means something about their ability, not just about that particular topic on that particular day.

Once "I got this wrong" becomes "I am bad at this," the child's goal quietly shifts. It stops being about learning the material and starts being about protecting themselves from further evidence. Volunteering an answer becomes risky, because a wrong answer now confirms the fact rather than just being a mistake. So they stop volunteering. Tests come home unmentioned, or not at all.

## Why encouragement alone doesn't undo it

"You're not bad at maths, you're brilliant" is a loving thing to say and it rarely changes anything, because your child is not weighing your opinion against the evidence — they are weighing their own experience of getting things wrong against your reassurance, and their own experience feels more reliable to them. A settled belief does not move because someone else disagrees with it. It moves when new evidence contradicts it directly, and when the fear of being wrong stops being quite so costly.

## One small thing to try this week

Instead of praising ability ("you're so clever"), try praising the specific moment of trying something they were not sure about: "You had a go at that even though you weren't certain — that's exactly the bit that gets easier with practice." This does two things. It separates effort from ability, so a wrong answer stops being proof of anything permanent. And it gives them a version of themselves to hold onto that survives being wrong — "I am someone who has a go," rather than "I am someone who is good or bad at this."

Give this weeks, not days. A belief that formed over a year or two does not reverse in one homework session.

## The same pattern, a different age

This is the same story that later shows up as a teenager choosing not to revise, so a low grade never quite counts. The belief calcifies with age; the earlier it is caught, the more it can still move.

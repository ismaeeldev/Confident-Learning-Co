You know the page. The one with the hole worn through it, right in the middle of a word that has been written and erased three or four times. Pencil shavings on the desk. A child who has gone quiet in a way that is somehow louder than shouting.

It is easy to read this as carelessness, or as a child who "just needs to concentrate." It is neither. A child who rubs through the page is not failing to try. They are trying so hard to get it right the first time that any visible mistake feels unbearable, so they erase it before you can see it — and often before they can quite bear to see it themselves.

## What is actually happening

Somewhere between reception and Year 4, most children work out that adults notice mistakes. Some children take that information and shrug it off. Others take it and quietly conclude that a mistake is not just a mistake — it is evidence. Evidence that they are not clever, or not good at this, or not like the other children who seem to get it first time.

Once a child believes that, the exercise book becomes a small, private battleground. Writing the word down is not the risk. Being seen to get it wrong is the risk. So they erase, and erase, and sometimes erase hard enough to tear straight through the paper, because in that moment the hole in the page feels safer than the wrong word.

## Why "just try your best" doesn't land

Most of us respond to a rubbed-through page with some version of "it doesn't matter if it's wrong, just try your best." It is well meant, and it almost never works, because the child was already trying their best. The problem was never effort. The problem is what a visible mistake means to them.

Telling a child who is afraid of being wrong that being wrong is fine does not remove the fear. It just tells them you have not quite understood what they are afraid of.

## One small thing to try this week

Instead of responding to the hole in the page, try responding to what came before it. Sit beside them for the next piece of homework and, before they write anything, say something like: "I bet you get some of these wrong. I do too. Let's find out which ones together." Then, when a mistake does appear, resist the urge to fix it immediately. Let it sit on the page for a moment. Name it plainly — "ah, that one didn't work" — without your voice changing, without a flicker of disappointment, and move on to the next line.

What you are doing is quietly proving, over and over, that a visible mistake does not change how you feel about them. That takes longer than one homework session to land. It is not a script to perform once. It is a pattern to repeat until the erasing stops being necessary.

## The same pattern, a different age

This is not really a homework problem. It is the same pattern that, at fifteen, looks like a teenager who stops revising altogether rather than revise and still get a low mark. Different age, same underlying logic: if you do not really try, a bad result never means anything about you.

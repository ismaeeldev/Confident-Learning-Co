It only ever happens on a school morning. By Saturday, your child is bouncing off the walls, appetite fully restored, no mention of their stomach at all. It is tempting to feel a flicker of suspicion (are they putting it on?) and then to feel guilty for thinking it. Neither reaction gets you very far.

## The body is telling the truth, even when the story isn't

A child who says their tummy hurts before school is not usually lying to you. The discomfort is often real. What is happening underneath it is that their body has started reacting to the anticipation of a specific feeling: the one that arrives at the point in the school day where they expect to feel stuck, wrong, or exposed. The stomach is simply where a six or seven year old's body sends that dread, because they do not yet have the words "I am dreading this" readily available to them.

This is different from a child who is being bullied or is unwell, both of which deserve their own direct attention. This is a narrower, more common pattern: a child who has quietly decided that some part of the school day (often literacy, or maths, or a specific lesson where they feel behind) is where they get caught out.

## Why "you'll be fine, off you go" doesn't work

It is the instinct of almost every loving parent to reassure and move things along, because the school run has a hard deadline and there is genuinely nothing wrong medically. But "you'll be fine" answers a question your child did not ask. They were not asking whether their tummy would recover. They were telling you, in the only language available to them, that something at school feels unsafe.

## One small thing to try this week

Rather than addressing the tummy ache directly, try addressing the morning before it starts. The evening before, ask one specific, low-pressure question: "Is there a bit of tomorrow you're not looking forward to?" Not "how was maths," which invites a shrug: a direct, gentle question about tomorrow specifically. If they name something, however small it sounds to you, take it seriously rather than reassuring it away. "That sounds like the tricky bit. What happens right before that lesson?"

You are not trying to solve the whole problem in one conversation. You are trying to show them that naming the dreaded thing out loud does not make anything worse, which is often exactly what a young child fears will happen if they say it.

## The same pattern, a different age

The tummy ache before school and the older sibling who "forgets" their PE kit on the day of a test are doing the same job: avoiding the moment where something might be seen to go wrong. The body just gets better at hiding it as children get older.
